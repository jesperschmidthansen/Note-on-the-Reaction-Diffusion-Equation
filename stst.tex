
An important class of problems deal with systems in the steady-state \index{Steady state}, where the
systems after a time transient no longer change with respect to time. The steady-state we formally define as  
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial c}{\partial t}=0 \ ,
\end{equation}
and we highlight the time independence by writing $c=c(x)$. In the steady-state  
the RD-equation reduces to an ordinary differential equation
\begin{equation}
	\frac{\d}{\d x} \left[ D \frac{\d c}{\d x}\right] + r(c)=0 \, .  
\end{equation}
This equation together with some appropriate BCs define a boundary value 
problem and is what we will study in this chapter. In the last optional section we 
also explore \emph{chemotaxis}\index{Chemotaxis} which is a phenomenon where cells migrate due to a 
signaling compound. While this is not a reaction-diffusion phenomenon, we can 
easily apply what we learn in the first sections and thus further extent the applications.

The boundary value problems we encounter here when $r$ is a linear function of $c$ 
are examples of the \emph{Sturm-Liouville} problem \index{Sturm-Liouville}. The theoretical 
framework of this general problem is elegant and quite useful, however, we shall 
not embark on an indepth treatment here, but simply make a few references. 


\section{Constant coefficients and linear reaction functions \label{sect:stst:BVP}}
In Example \ref{ex:simplegrowth} we introduced an RD-equation with a simple 
linear reaction function, $r=kc$. Let us explore this example, specifically for the case 
where $k>0$ and $D>0$ are constants  
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:simplegrowthss}
	D\frac{\d^2c}{\d x^2} + kc=0 \, .
\end{equation}
Since we have no time in the problem we do not have an IC, and in this first
example we apply simple Dirichlet BCs
\begin{equation}
  c(0)=c(L)=0 \, .
\end{equation}
Equation (\ref{eq:simplegrowthss}) is a homogeneous second order linear equation with 
constant coefficients. The \emph{eigenvalues} \index{Eigenvalue} for this equation are found from the characteristic
equation \footnote{If you need to refresh this, I recommend that you go the exercises for this section.}
\begin{equation}
	\lambda^2 + \frac{k}{D} = 0
	\label{eq:eigInfinite}
\end{equation}
implying eigenvalues $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$ 
\begin{equation}
 \lambda_{1,2} = \pm i \sqrt{\frac{k}{D}}   \, ,
\end{equation}
because both coefficients are positive. Since we have distinct eigenvalues 
the general solution is a linear combination of two exponential functions . Therefore 
by application of Euler's identity we get
\begin{eqnarray}
	c(x) &=& Ae^{\lambda_1 x} + Be^{\lambda_2 x} = A e^{i \sqrt{k/D} \, x} + B e^{-i \sqrt{k/D} \, x}
           \nonumber \\
       &=& (A+B)\cos\left(\sqrt{k/D}\,x\right) +
           i(A-B)\sin\left(\sqrt{k/D}\,x\right) \, ,
		   \label{eq:stsgeneral2order}
\end{eqnarray}
where $A$ and $B$ are constants we must seek from the BCs. 

From the left BC (at $x=0$) we get
\begin{equation}
  c(0) = A + B = 0 \ \Rightarrow \ A = -B   \, ,
\end{equation}
and using this result together with the right BC (at $x=L$) we have
\begin{equation}
  c(L) = i2A\sin\left(\sqrt{k/D}\,L\right) = 0 \, .
\end{equation}
This relation is of course fulfilled for $A=0$ and this leads to the 
\emph{trivial solution} \index{Trivial solution} $c=0$ for all $x \in [0;L]$. Perhaps more interestingly, the equation is also 
fulfilled when $A\neq 0$ and $\sin(\sqrt{k/D}\,L ) = 0$, that is, for
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:constraintL}
	\sqrt{\frac{k}{D}} \, L = n\pi \, , \ \text{where} \ n \in \mathbb{N}_+ \, .
\end{equation}
Note that since $k, D$ and $L$ are all postive reel numbers, the right-hand side must be positive 
implying that $n$ is a positive integer as we indicate by $\mathbb{N}_+$. Also, notice that for a
given set $k, D$ and $L$ we may have $\sqrt{k/D} L \neq n \pi$, hence, only the trivial solution
exists. 

Assume that Equation(\ref{eq:constraintL}) is fulfilled, then we can substitute the relation into the 
general solution, Equation (\ref{eq:stsgeneral2order}), obtaining    
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:gensolfirst}
c(x) = i2A \sin\left(\frac{n \pi}{L} \, x \right)  \, .
\end{equation}
In our treatment here $c$ is a real valued function and therefore $A$ must be imaginary. Moreover, if 
we demand $c \geq 0$ ($c$ representing concentration) only $n=1$  will work, defining the length    
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:L0}
  L_0 = \pi  \sqrt{\frac{D}{k}} \, ,
\end{equation}
constraining the $x$-coordinate to $0 \leq x \leq L_0$. 

We see that even if we have two BCs we cannot determine the constant $A$ (and get a unique solution). 
What we got from the BCs is the relation between the constants $A$ and $B$, 
and the non-trivial solution constraint given in Equation (\ref{eq:constraintL}). 
We must conclude that there exists infinitely many solutions. 

The result here, namely, that there does not exist a unique solution may be 
surprising if we consider the corresponding
experimental bio-chemical system we seek to model. The substance, say, a plankton population
features a self-multiplying process, and due to the concentration gradient,
the produced plankton is transported via diffusion to the tube-ends where it is
removed (how, will not concern us here). Now, if the diffusive transport 
is sufficiently fast compared to the production, the population vanishes with time 
(as the plankton is removed at the tube ends), and we simply end in the trivial solution $c=0$. 
On the other hand, if diffusion cannot transport the produced plankton to the tube ends fast enough, the concentration
will keep raising leading to $c$ diverging at all interior points; and now we must of course evaluate whether our
model fulfill its purpose. Finally, there is also a state where there exists a 
(strict) balance between the diffusive transport out of the system and the production, given by $\sqrt{k/D} = \pi/L_0$, 
and this allows for a non-trivial solution. \label{page:nouniquness} In Chapter \ref{ch:rd} we revisit these points in more detail. 

Let us briefly return to the solutions in Equation (\ref{eq:gensolfirst}). The \emph{super-position
principle for linear systems}  \index{Super-Position principle} 
states that if Equation (\ref{eq:gensolfirst}) are solutions to the problem, then a
linear combination of these solutions is also a solution. Therefore, we have, in general, the series solution
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:2ndorderseries}
	c(x) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty b_n \sin\left(\frac{n\pi}{L} \, x \right) \, ,
\end{equation}
where $b_n \in \R$ are constants. This general solution can be understood in a more abstract manner. To this
end, first note (or recall) that the sine function is a vector. For example, the distributive law holds for 
the standard addition operator 
\begin{eqnarray}
	\label{eq:sinevector}
	(a + b) \sin\left(\frac{n \pi}{L} \, x\right) = a\sin\left(\frac{n\pi}{L} \, x\right) + b \sin\left(\frac{n\pi}{L} \, x\right) 
\end{eqnarray}
where $a$ and $b$ are scalars; the other axioms are just as easily verified. Secondly, the functions
$\sin(\pi/L \, x), \sin(2\pi/L \, x), \sin(3\pi/L \, x), \ldots$ are \emph{linearly independent}
\index{Linear independence} 
because for $n,m \in \mathbb{N}_+$
\begin{eqnarray}
	a \sin\left(\frac{n \pi}{L} \, x\right) + b\sin\left(\frac{m \pi}{L} \, x\right) = 0 \, , \ x \in
	[0; L] \, ,
\end{eqnarray}
implies that $a=b=0$.  Thus, $\sin(\pi/L \, x), \sin(2\pi/L \, x),
\sin(3\pi/L \, x), \ldots$ span an infinite dimensional vector space, and the solution $c$ is simply a
vector itself and represented in this basis by the coefficients $b_n$. 

\begin{question}
	Can you verify the statement in Equation (\ref{eq:2ndorderseries})? (Hint: Show
	Equation (\ref{eq:2ndorderseries}) is solution to Equation (\ref{eq:simplegrowthss})
	with the BCs $c(0)=c(L)=0$.)
\end{question}

Finally, some terminology: We can rewrite the relation in Equation (\ref{eq:constraintL}) slightly 
\begin{eqnarray}
	\sqrt{\frac{k}{D}} = \frac{n\pi}{L} \, .
\end{eqnarray}
Comparing this with Equation (\ref{eq:eigInfinite}) this motivates us to name $n\pi/L$ an eigenvalue, and
we some times assign it the symbol
$\lambda_n$, where $n$ refers to the natural number in the nominator. Note, for this problem we have 
infinitely many eigenvalues where $\lambda_n < \lambda_m$  if $n<m$. The solution for a given 
eigenvalue is called an \emph{eigenfunction} \index{Eigenfunction}\index{Function!eigen-}; here the sine functions are eigenfunctions. 
The trivial solution, $c=0$, is not an eigenfunction.

\noindent Let us see two more examples.

\begin{example} \label{ex:stst:polsol}
	In case the production function is a constant we have an inhomogeneous
	equation on the form
	\begin{equation}
		\frac{\d^2c}{\d x^2} = K \ , \ \ K\in \R.
	\end{equation}
	We will apply mixed boundaries
	\begin{equation}
		c(0)=0 \ \ \text{and} \ \ \left.\frac{\d c}{\d x}\right|_{x=L}=1 \, .
	\end{equation}

	\begin{wrapfigure}{l}{0.4\textwidth}
		\includegraphics[width=0.35\textwidth]{figs/solutionEx4.eps}
	\end{wrapfigure}
	
	The experimental realisation of these BCs is of no importance to us here. 

	Clearly, the exponential function cannot be a solution to this problem. 
	Now, let $y=\d c/\d x$, then	$\d y/\d x = K$, implying that $y = Kx + A$ and therefore
	\begin{equation}
		c(x) = \frac{K}{2}x^2 + Ax + B \, .
	\end{equation}
	From the left BC we get $B=0$, and since 
	\begin{equation}
	 \left.\frac{\d c}{\d x}\right|_{x=L}= KL + A = 1 \ \Rightarrow \ A = 1 - KL
	\end{equation}
	the solution is 
	\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:solutionEx4}
	 c(x) = \frac{K}{2}x^2 + (1-KL)x 
	\end{equation}
	This was less painful; in summary, the solution exists and is unqiue. Again, we can think 
	of $c$ as a vector; this time the vector space is spanned by polynomials.  
%	\marginpar{\includegraphics[scale=0.255555]{figs/solutionEx4.eps}}i
\end{example}

\begin{example}
  We again consider the simple linear growth problem 
  \begin{equation}
    D\frac{\d^2c}{\d x^2} + kc=0 \, , 
  \end{equation}
  but this time we will apply the BCs
  \begin{equation}
    c(0) = 1 \ , \ c(L_0) = 1 \, .
  \end{equation}
  $L_0$ is given in Equation (\ref{eq:L0}).  Now, the general solution is the same
	as above, Equation (\ref{eq:stsgeneral2order}), 
	and from the first BC we obtain the relation $B=1-A$. From the second BC we have 
  \begin{equation}
    c(L_0)=\cos(\pi) + i(2A-1)\sin(\pi) = -1 \, .
  \end{equation}
	This conflicts with the right BC and is and example of an \emph{ill posed problem} \index{Ill
	posed problem}. Both BCs cannot be satisfied and no solution exists.
\end{example}

The examples show that we need to be careful when dealing with boundary value problems. Even if the differential 
equation looks harmless and the general solution exists it may be an ill posed problem or 
have infinitely many solutions. We cannot transfer the existence and uniqueness theorems from initial value 
problems to boundary value problems. In summary, we have the following possible outcomes:
\begin{itemize}
\item There does not exist a solution.
\item There exists one unique solution. 
\item There exist infinitely many solutions.
\end{itemize}
We will return to existence and uniqueness in the next section.

In this section, we have only dealt with the case where both $k$ and $D$ are constants and
independent of position. Clearly, this need not to be the case, e.g., 
the substance can be distributed in an inhomogeneous medium. These types of
problems calls for a different approach, which we will treat in Section \ref{sec:inhomogenousstst}.

\section{Existence and uniqueness of solutions} \label{sect:uniq}
As illustrated in the previous section, we are not guaranteed a unique or even a single solution 
to a given boundary value problem. In some cases we can give a formal proof if a solution exists to 
and whether it is unique. For example, consider the famous one dimensional \emph{Laplace equation}
\index{Laplace!equation}
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:laplace}
  \frac{\d^2 c}{\d x^2} =  0 \, . 
\end{equation}
 
\begin{theorem}
  The only solution to the Laplace equation with Dirichlet BCs
  \begin{equation}
    c(0) = c(L)=0
  \end{equation}
  is the trivial solution $c=0$.
\end{theorem}
Before going through the formal proof we can quite easily understand why
this is likely true. A good guess for the general solution to the Laplace equation is a
simple linear function on the form $c = ax + b$, where $a$ and $b$
are the coefficients we must find from the BCs. The condition $c(0) = 0$ implies that $b=0$,
and only $a=0$ then satisfies $c(L) = 0$, that is, $c=0$.

\begin{question}
	Why should we not consider this reasoning as a satisfactory proof for existence and uniqueness?
\end{question}

\begin{proof}
	Assume two solutions exists, namely, $\varphi_1$ and $\varphi_2$. We 
	define a third function, $\varphi$,  as the difference between these two solutions 
	$\varphi = \varphi_1 - \varphi_2$. We will quickly see $\varphi$ is a solution and that $\varphi = 0$  
	the only solution. 

	First, $\varphi$ fulfills the Laplace equation with same Dirichlet BCs as the original problem since
	\begin{equation}
		\label{eq:laplacevarphi}
		 \frac{\d^2 \varphi}{\d x^2} =  \frac{\d^2 \varphi_1}{\d x^2} -  \frac{\d^2 \varphi_2}{\d
		 x^2} = 0  \, ,
	\end{equation}
	as well as $\varphi(0) = \varphi_1(0) - \varphi_2(0)=0$ and $\varphi(L)=\varphi_1(L) -
	\varphi_2(L)=0$. From the product rule we have the following identity
	\begin{equation}
    	\frac{\d}{\d x}\left(\varphi \frac{\d \varphi}{\d x} \right) = \left( \frac{\d \varphi}{\d
        	x}\right)^2 + \varphi \frac{\d^2 \varphi}{\d x^2} = \left( \frac{\d \varphi}{\d x}\right)^2 \, ,
  \end{equation}
  where the last relation is due to Equation (\ref{eq:laplacevarphi}). We integrate
	both sides; from the fundamental theorem of calculus the left-hand side
	becomes 
  \begin{equation}
    \int_0^L \frac{\d}{\d x}\left(\varphi \frac{\d \varphi}{\d x} \right) \, \d x  =
    \left.  \varphi \frac{\d  \varphi}{\d x} \right|_{x=0}^{x=L} = 0 
  \end{equation}
  from the BCs. This implies that the right-hand is also zero, i.e.,
  \begin{equation}
	  \int_0^L \left( \frac{\d  \varphi} {\d x}\right)^2 \, \d x = 0  \, .
  \end{equation}
  The integrand is always zero or positive because it is a real function squared, 
	thus, for the integral to be zero we
  must have $\d  \varphi /\d x = 0$ for $0 \leq x \leq L$. The only way the BCs can be
  satisfied is then if
  \begin{equation}
     \varphi  = \varphi(0)= \varphi(L)=0 \ \text{for}  \ 0 \leq x \leq L \, . 
  \end{equation}
	This means that $\varphi_1=\varphi_2$ (only one solution exists), 
	and since $\varphi$ is given by the original Laplace equation we have $\phi=c$ and only the 
	trivial solution is the solution.
\end{proof}

\noindent Things change dramatically if we change the BCs. 
\begin{theorem}
  The Laplace equation with Neumann BCs
  \begin{equation}
    \frac{\d^2c}{\d x^2} = 0 \ , \ \text{with} \  
    \left.\frac{\d c}{\d x}\right|_{x=0}=\left.\frac{\d c}{\d x}\right|_{x=L} =0 \, ,   
  \end{equation}
  does not have a unique solution.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
	The proof follows the same idea as above and is left to the reader 
	(Come to class if you are stuck!)
\end{proof}

Except from the exercises below, we will not pursue proving these 
existence and uniqueness theorems further; again the interested reader can consult the literature on this.

\section{Varying coefficients: Series solutions \label{sec:inhomogenousstst}\index{Series!solutions}}
In Section \ref{sect:stst:BVP} we studied the case where $D$ and $k$ are constants. In general, this 
is not the case. For example, in a high column of water the biological growth rate coefficient can vary with the depth 
as the amount of sunlight varies, and temperature variations in the system will affect the
diffusion coefficient. The position dependency we highlight by writing $D=D(x)$ and $k=k(x)$. For
varying coeffcients the steady-state RD-equation is on the form   
\begin{eqnarray}
	\label{eq:stst:nonconstcoeff}
	D(x) \frac{\d^2c}{\d x^2} + D'(x) \frac{\d c}{\d x} + k(x) c = 0 \, ,
\end{eqnarray}
using the product rule and where $D'(x) = \d D(x)/\d x$. From our definition of a diffusion process
we have $D(x) > 0$ for $x \in [0; L]$ and Equation (\ref{eq:stst:nonconstcoeff}) is often rewritten on
the form
\begin{equation}
	\frac{\d^2 c}{\d x^2} + p(x)\frac{\d c}{\d x} + q(x) c = 0
\end{equation}
with $p(x)=D'(x)/D(x)$ and $q(x)=k(x)/D(x)$. 

The solutions for $c$ for these problems are usually not given by low order polynomials or 
linear combinations of trigonometric and exponential functions, and, in fact, the solution can be an unknown function. 
The strategy is to find the solution in terms of its power series representation 
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:stst:ssol}
	c(x) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty c_n (x-x_0)^n \ , 
\end{equation}
where $x_0$ is some point (typically in the interval $[0;L]$), and $c_n$ are coefficients we need to
determine.  \index{Series!power} For this to be meaningful in the first place we must have that the limit of the 
\emph{the partial sum} \index{Partial sum}
\begin{eqnarray}
	s_m(x) = \sum_{n=0}^m c_n (x-x_0)^n 
\end{eqnarray}
exists at $x$ as $m \rightarrow \infty$. If this is so, we say the series \emph{converges pointwise}
\index{Convergence!pointwise}
at the point $x$ and that $c(x) = \lim_{m\rightarrow \infty} s_m(x)$. A formal definition of
pointwise convergence is based on an $\epsilon-N$ definition: Let $c: S \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$; 
then the series converges pointwise 	
to $c$ on $S$ if for all $\epsilon > 0$ there exists an $N(x, \epsilon) \in \mathbb{N}$ 
such that $|s_m(x) - c(x)| < \epsilon$ for all $x \in S$ and $m \geq N$. Later we define a 
"stronger" type of convergence using an $\epsilon-N$ definition, but we shall not go into any 
details.   


We first list a very powerful result which we will not prove. In our treatment here $D$ and $k$ are polynomials
and since $D \neq 0$ in any point in the interval $[0; L]$, the points are \emph{ordinary
points}\index{Ordinary point}.  
If a point is not an ordinary point it is a \emph{singular point}\index{Singular point}, and this occurs for points where
$D=0$. The functions $p$ and $q$ are not defined in singular points. 
Now, if $x_0$ is an ordinary point we are guarantied a general solution on the form of a 
power series, Equation (\ref{eq:stst:ssol}), in some neighbourhood around the point $x_0$. 
The idea is simple: if $D$ and $k$ are "well behaved" we should expect that the general solution 
to the differential equation exists and, furthermore, that the solution can be represented by a power series.
Of course, we may still have an ill posed boundary value problem prohibiting a particular solution.  

\bigskip 

\noindent To illustrate the method, we revisit the special case where diffusion and growth rate are 
constants, i.e., they are zero'th order polynomials. Recall, this case we first 
treated in Section \ref{sect:stst:BVP}. We have 
\begin{eqnarray}
	\label{eq:stst:constantcoefrev}
	D\frac{\d^2 c}{\d x^2} + k c = 0 \ \implies \frac{\d^2c}{\d x^2} =  -\lambda c = 0 \ ,
\end{eqnarray}
where $\lambda = k/D > 0$ since both rate and diffision coefficents are constants. 

First, we notice that $p=0$ and $q=\lambda$ so any point is an ordinary point and we are guarantied a
general solution around $x_0 \in \R$. We will (usually) choose the left boudary $x_0=0$, hence, we look 
for a series solution on the form
\begin{eqnarray}
	\label{eq:stst:seriesx00}
	c(x) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty c_n x^n \, .
\end{eqnarray}
We may now substitute Equation ($\ref{eq:stst:seriesx00})$ into Equation (\ref{eq:stst:constantcoefrev})
and compare terms in order to extract the coefficients $c_n$. Since this is an ordinary
problem and we can differentiate term wise, thus, differentiating once  
\begin{eqnarray}
	\label{eq:stst:dseries}
	\frac{\d c}{\d x} = c_1 + 2c_2x + 3c_3x^2 + 4c_4 x^3 + \ldots = \sum_{n=1}^\infty n c_n x^{n-1}
\end{eqnarray}
and twice 
\begin{eqnarray}
	\label{eq:stst:d2series}
	\frac{\d^2c}{\d x^2} = 2c_2 + 6c_3x + 12c_4x^2 + \ldots = \sum_{n=2}^\infty n(n-1) c_n
	x^{n-2} \, .
\end{eqnarray}
Before the substitution it is often practical if the starting indices are the same; we can
achieve this by defining a shifting index, here denoted $m$. For the 1st order derivative 
we let  $m=n-1$ and therefore $n=m+1$, and Equation (\ref{eq:stst:dseries}) is written as
\begin{eqnarray}
	\label{eq:stst:dindexshift}
	\frac{\d c}{\d x} = \sum_{n=1}^\infty n c_n x^{n-1}=\sum_{m=0}^\infty (m+1) c_{m+1}x^m \, .
\end{eqnarray}
For the 2nd order derivative we let $m=n-2$. Then $n=m+2$ and 
\begin{eqnarray}
	\label{eq:stst:d2indexshift}
	\frac{\d^2c}{\d x^2}= \sum_{n=2}^\infty n(n-1) c_n x^{n-2} = \sum_{m=0}^\infty (m+2)(m+1)
	c_{m+2} x^m \, .
\end{eqnarray}
The index symbol is, of course, changeable so we can simply rename $m$ to $n$. Substitution we have
\begin{eqnarray}
	\sum_{n=0}^\infty (n+2)(n+1)c_{n+2}x^n = -\lambda \sum_{n=0}^\infty c_n x^n \, . 
\end{eqnarray}
This relation is true if each term in the two series are equal, that is, if
\begin{eqnarray}
	(n+2)(n+1)c_{n+2} = -\lambda c_n \ \implies  \ c_{n+2} = -\frac{\lambda c_n}{(n+2)(n+1)} \, .
\end{eqnarray}
This is the \emph{recurrence relation}\index{Recurrence relation}. From the recurrence relation we can determine all
coefficients if we know $c_0$ and $c_1$
\begin{eqnarray}
	c_2 = -\frac{\lambda c_0}{1\cdot 2} \ , \ c_3 = -\frac{\lambda c_1}{2\cdot 3} \ , \ 
	c_4 = -\frac{\lambda c_2}{3\cdot4} = \frac{\lambda^2c_0}{1\cdot2\cdot3\cdot4} \  , \ 
	c_5 = -\frac{\lambda c_3}{4\cdot5} = \frac{\lambda^2c_1}{2\cdot3\cdot4\cdot5}  \nonumber 
\end{eqnarray}
and so on. Notice that $c_0$ appears in the terms where $n$ is even, and $c_1$ in the terms where $n$ is
odd. We can then write the general solution on the form of an even series and an odd series 
\begin{eqnarray}
	c(x) &=& c_0\left(1 - \frac{\lambda}{2!}x^2 + \frac{\lambda^2}{4!}x^4 - \ldots\right) 
			+ c_1 \left(x- \frac{\lambda}{3!}x^3 + \frac{\lambda^2}{5!}x^5 - \ldots\right) 
			\nonumber \\
			&=& c_0 \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{(-1)^n \lambda^n}{2n!}x^{2n} + c_1 \sum_{n=0}^\infty 
			\frac{(-1)^n \lambda^n}{(2n+1)!}x^{2n+1} \, .
\end{eqnarray}
This is just a linear combination of the Taylor series for cosine and sine
\begin{eqnarray}
	\frac{\cos(\sqrt{\lambda}x)}{\sqrt{\lambda}} &=&  \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{(-1)^n \lambda^n}{2n!}x^{2n} 
	\nonumber	\\
	\frac{\sin(\sqrt{\lambda}x)}{\sqrt{\lambda}} &=&   \sum_{n=0}^\infty 
			\frac{(-1)^n \lambda^n}{(2n+1)!}x^{2n+1} 
\end{eqnarray}
so we have the final from
\begin{eqnarray}
	c(x) = \frac{c_0}{\sqrt{\lambda}} \cos(\sqrt{\lambda} x) 
	+ \frac{c_1}{\sqrt{\lambda}} \sin(\sqrt{\lambda} x)
\end{eqnarray}
and we have same result as we obtained in Equation (\ref{eq:stsgeneral2order}), which we
should also expect, of course. The coefficients can, some times, be determined by BCs.    

The fact that the solution's power series is the Taylor series is a general results. So, even if the
solution is an unknown function we can derive the function's Taylor series (and give it a name 
if we wish). 

\begin{example} \label{ex:stst:ss_1}
	We consider the case where the diffusion coefficient is constant, $D(x)=D$ 
	and	where the growth rate coefficient is linear with respect to space $k(x)= k_0 x$. Then, 
	\begin{equation}
		\label{eq:stst:exampless}
		D\frac{\d^2 c}{\d x^2} + k_0 x c = 0 \ \implies \ \frac{\d^2 c}{\d x^2} = -\lambda x c  
	\end{equation}
	where $ \lambda = k_0/D$. We use the BCs 
	\begin{eqnarray}
		c(0)=0 \text{ and } c(1)=1 \, .
	\end{eqnarray}
	We have that $p=0$ and $q=\lambda x$, so any point $x_0$ is an ordinary point and  
	we are guarantied a solution in a neighborhood around $x_0$. 
	Let us choose the left boundary $x_0=0$; we seek the coefficients for the series 
	\begin{eqnarray}
		\label{eq:stst:ssol_x00}
		c(x) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty c_n x^n \, .
	\end{eqnarray}
	Now, we substitute the series Equation. (\ref{eq:stst:ssol_x00}) and (\ref{eq:stst:d2indexshift}) 
	- with index symbol $n$ - into	Equation(\ref{eq:stst:exampless}) we arrive at
	\begin{eqnarray}
		 \sum_{n=0}^\infty (n+2)(n+1)c_{n+2} x^n + \lambda x \sum_{n=0}^\infty c_n x^n = 0  
	\end{eqnarray}
	or by distributing $x$ in the last term
	\begin{eqnarray}
		\sum_{n=0}^\infty (n+2)(n+1)c_{n+2} x^n + \lambda \sum_{n=0}^\infty c_n x^{n+1} = 0 \, . 
	\end{eqnarray}
	It is a bit harder to derive the recurrence relation for this case. It may be deduced by writing
	out a few terms; we obtain
	\begin{eqnarray}
		x^0 &:& 2c_2 = 0 \ \implies \ c_2 = 0 \nonumber \\
		x^1 &:& 3\cdot2 c_3 + \lambda c_0 = 0 \ \implies \ c_3 = -\lambda c_0/(3 \cdot 2) \nonumber \\
		x^2 &:& 4\cdot3 c_4 + \lambda c_1 = 0 \ \implies \ c_4 = -\lambda c_1/(4 \cdot 3) \nonumber \\
		x^3 &:& 5\cdot4 c_5 + \lambda c_2 = 0 \ \implies \ c_5 = 0 \nonumber \\
		x^4 &:& 6\cdot5 c_6 + \lambda c_3 = 0 \ \implies \ c_6 = -\lambda c_3/(6\cdot5) \nonumber \\
		x^6 &:& 7\cdot6 c_7 + \lambda c_4 = 0 \ \implies \ c_7 = -\lambda c_4/(7\cdot6) \nonumber \\
		&\vdots& \nonumber
	\end{eqnarray}
	Clearly, there is a structure 
	\begin{eqnarray}
		c_2 = 0 \text{ and } c_n = -\frac{\lambda c_{n-3}}{n(n-1)} \text{ for } n \geq 3 \, .
	\end{eqnarray}
	Notice that since $c_2=0$ we have that $c_5=c_8=c_{11}=\ldots=0$. Moreover, from the recurrence
	relation we get
	\begin{eqnarray}
		c_6 &=& -\frac{\lambda c_3}{6\cdot5} =  \frac{\lambda^2c_0}{6\cdot5\cdot3\cdot2} \nonumber \\  
		c_7 &=& -\frac{\lambda c_4}{7\cdot6} = \frac{\lambda^2c_1}{7\cdot6\cdot4\cdot3} \nonumber 
	\end{eqnarray}
	and 
	\begin{eqnarray}
		c_9 &=& -\frac{\lambda c_6}{6\cdot5} = -\frac{\lambda^3c_0}{9\cdot8\cdot6\cdot5\cdot3\cdot2}
		\nonumber \\
		c_{10} &=& -\frac{\lambda c_7}{7\cdot6} = -\frac{\lambda^3c_1}{10\cdot9\cdot7\cdot6\cdot4\cdot3} 
		\nonumber
	\end{eqnarray}
	and so forth. 
	Again, this leaves only two unknowns, namely, $c_0$ and $c_1$, and we again write
	the general solution as a linear combiniation of two series 
	\begin{eqnarray}
		c(x) &=& c_0 \left(1 - \frac{\lambda}{3\cdot2}x^3 + \frac{\lambda^2}{6\cdot5\cdot3\cdot2}x^6 -
		\frac{\lambda^3}{9\cdot8\cdot6\cdot5\cdot3\cdot2}x^9 + \ldots \right) \nonumber \\
		&+& c_1 \left(
			x - \frac{\lambda}{4\cdot3}x^4 + \frac{\lambda^2}{7\cdot6\cdot4\cdot3}x^7 -
			\frac{\lambda^3}{10\cdot9\cdot7\cdot6\cdot4\cdot3}x^{10} + \ldots \right)
		\label{eq:stst:ssolEx1}
	\end{eqnarray}
	In practice we cannot not add up infinite terms, and some times we may not even 
	be able to find a general recursion relation. We therefore often use the partial sum
	\begin{eqnarray}
		s_m(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{m} c_n x^n \, .
	\end{eqnarray}
	Applying the left boundary condition, $c(0)=0$, we get $c_0=0$ no matter the choice of 
	truncation and then up to, say 10th order, we have 
	\begin{equation}
		c(x) \approx c_1 s_{10}(x) = c_1 \left(
			x - \frac{\lambda}{4\cdot3}x^4 + \frac{\lambda^2}{7\cdot6\cdot4\cdot3}x^7 - 
			\frac{\lambda^3}{10\cdot9\cdot7\cdot6\cdot4\cdot3}x^{10} \right)
	\end{equation}
	Here we determine $c_1$ from the partial sum: since $c(1)\approx c_1 s_{10}(1)=1$ we
	get $c_1 = 1/s_{10}(1)$. Different partial sums are plotted for the case $\lambda = 10$. Notice that already after
	three terms, or 7th order in $x$, we see a good approximation.
\end{example}

\noindent Recall, we are only guarantied a series solution in some neighbourhood around the ordinary
point $x_0$. Formally, we quantify this neighbourhood by the series' \emph{pointwise convergence
radius}\index{Convergence!radius}, $R$,  
that is, the solution is guarantied for points that fulfill $0 < |x - x_0| < R$. The convergence radius 
can, some times,  be found from the famous \emph{ratio test}\index{Ratio test}. For the power series the ratio test states 
that the series    
\begin{eqnarray}
	\label{eq:stst:ratiotest}
	& &	\text{convergences if } |x-x_0| \lim_{n \rightarrow \infty} \left| \frac{c_{n+1}}{c_n} \right| < 1 
	 \nonumber \\ & & \\
	& &\text{divergence if } |x-x_0| \lim_{n \rightarrow \infty} \left| \frac{c_{n+1}}{c_n} \right|
	> 1 \nonumber \\ & & 
\end{eqnarray}
for $c_n \neq 0$. The test is inconclusive for $|x-x_0| \lim_{n \rightarrow \infty} \left|
c_{n+1}/c_n \right| = 1$. Strictly, the ratio test tests for absolute convergence: 
if a function series converges absolutely it also converges pointwise. 

\begin{example}\label{ex:stst:convseries}
	The power series 
	\begin{eqnarray}
		c(x) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty 2^n x^n 
	\end{eqnarray}
	converges for $|x| < 1/2$. To see this note that $x_0=0$ and the ratio test gives
	\begin{eqnarray}
		|x| \lim_{n \rightarrow \infty} \left| \frac{2^{n+1}}{2^n} \right| = 
		|x| \lim_{n \rightarrow \infty} 2  = 2 |x| \, . 
	\end{eqnarray}
	Thus, for $|x| < 1/2$, or $-1/2 < x < 1/2$, this limit fulfills the relation in Equation
	(\ref{eq:stst:ratiotest}). The radius of convergence is 1/2.  
\end{example}

\end{eqnarray}
Recall $c_0=0$. It is understood that for $n=1$ we include one multiplication in the denominator, $n=2$

\begin{eqnarray}
	c_n = \frac{(-1)^n}{(3n+1)\cdot(3n)\ldots 4\cdot3} 
\end{eqnarray}
and for index $n+1$
\begin{eqnarray}
	c_{n+1} = \frac{(-1)^n}{(3(n+1)+1)\cdot(3(n+1))\cdot(3n+1)\cdot(3n))\ldots 4\cdot3} \, . 
\end{eqnarray}
This implies that
\begin{eqnarray}
	|x| \lim_{m \rightarrow \infty} \left| \frac{c_{n+1}}{c_n} \right| = |x| \lim_{n\rightarrow
	\infty }   \left| \frac{1}{(3(n+1)+1)\cdot(3(n+1))} \right| =  0 \, .
\end{eqnarray}
This limit is independent of $x$ and the series solution converges on $\R$. This implies that the power series solution 
found in Equation (\ref{eq:stst:ssolEx1}) is the solution for all $x$.

\section{Non-linear reaction functions}
Bio-chemical reactions are often non-linear. The first way to approach such situation is, almost always, to study the
system in some special cases or to do a linear analysis in hope this gives some qualitative
understanding of the system. Some times, however, we wish to extend our analysis, e.g., 
if we want a better quantitative output from the model. 

Consider the boundary value problem
\begin{equation}
  D\frac{\d^2c}{\d x^2} + k c^2 = 0 \ \ \text{with} \ \ 
  c(0) = 0 \ \ \text{and} \ \ c(1)=1 \, .
\end{equation}
Notice that the reaction term is non-linear and we cannot use the standard
techniques discussed so far to solve this. We can re-arrange this a bit by dividing with $D$, and introduce a 
new parameter $\epsilon = k/D$, $D \neq 0$, 
such that the dynamical equation becomes
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:second}
  \frac{\d^2c}{\d x^2} + \epsilon c^2 = 0 \, . 
\end{equation}
In the case where the diffusion coefficient is large compared to the rate coefficient, $D \gg k$, 
we have that $\epsilon$ is small. Indeed, we will expect that the solution in this case 
is ``close'' to the solution for $\d^2c/\d x^2 = 0$,  
where there is no reaction process. We can think of $\epsilon$ as a parameter that controls 
a (small) perturbation of the system away from the pure diffusion case. 
For this reason $\epsilon$ is called the \emph{perturbation parameter}\index{Perturbation!parameter} and Equation (\ref{eq:second}) 
forms a \emph{regular perturbation problem}\index{Perturbation!regular problem}. A non-regular problem is illustrated later.

Once we have defined a small system parameter and have a regular perturbation problem, 
we can attempt to do an analysis based on the \emph{regular perturbation method}. 
This method is based on the fundamental assumption (called an ansatz) that the function
$c$ can be represented by power series with respect to the perturbation parameter. 
At first this does not appear to be very helpful since we are interested in $c$ as a
function of the spatial coordinate $x$ and not the parameter $\epsilon$,
however, the power series will result in a, some times, tractable structure. The
regular perturbation method involves the following five steps:

\begin{enumerate}
\item{Assume that a solution $c=c(x)$ exists. Furthermore, assume that $c$ can
    be written in terms of a power series with respect to $\epsilon$
    \begin{equation}
      \label{eq:pertass1}
		c(x) = c_0(x) + \epsilon c_1(x) + \epsilon^2 c_2(x) + \epsilon^3c_3(x) + \ldots
    \end{equation}
    where $c_0$, $c_1$, \ldots are functions of $x$ and independent of
    $\epsilon$. We now hope that $c$ is "well-approximated" with only a 
    few terms, that is, we hope that the function series converges to
    the actual function quickly.
		}
\item{ 
    Insert the power series for $c$ into the dynamical equation for $c$,
    Equation (\ref{eq:second}),
    \begin{eqnarray}
      \frac{\d^2}{\d x^2} (c_0 + \epsilon c_1 + \epsilon^2 c_2 + \dots) +
      \epsilon(c_0 + \epsilon c_1 + \epsilon^2 c_2 + \dots)^2 = 0  \, .
    \end{eqnarray}
  }
\item{
    Collect term-wise with respect to powers of $\epsilon$. To fulfill the
	equation we equate all terms with zero, and letting $\epsilon \neq 0$ we
	obtain	
    \begin{eqnarray}
      &\epsilon^0:& \ \frac{\d^2 c_0}{\d x^2} = 0 \label{eq:p1}\\
      &\epsilon^1:& \ \frac{\d^2 c_1}{\d x^2} + c_0^2 = 0  \label{eq:p2}
      \\
      &\epsilon^2:& \ \frac{\d^2 c_2}{\d x^2} + 2c_0c_1 = 0 \\
      &\vdots& \nonumber
    \end{eqnarray}
	We see this results in a set of tractable problems (albeit in general quite tedious).
    From Equation (\ref{eq:p1}) we obtain $c_0$
   		(once the appropriate BCss are specified). This
    result is substituted into Equation (\ref{eq:p2}) and $c_1$ can be found
    (once the appropriate BCs are specified), and so
    forth. Notice that in this case each problem is linear.
  }
\item{
	Solve for $c_0, c_1, \ldots$
    
    Here we first need to define the BCs. We can let the unperturbed
    zero order problem, that is, for $\epsilon=0$, fulfill the original BCs is, we must have
    $c_0(0)=0$ and $c_0(1)=1$. This implies that $c_n(0)=0$ and $c_n(1)=0$,
    $n=1,2$,\ldots, because
		\begin{eqnarray}
			c(0) = c_0(0) + \epsilon c_1(0) + \epsilon^2 c_2(0) + \ldots = 0
		\end{eqnarray}
		and
		\begin{eqnarray}
			c(1) = c_0(1) + \epsilon c_1(1) + \epsilon^2 c_2(1) + \ldots = c_0(1)=1 
		\end{eqnarray}
    Then solving
    \begin{eqnarray}
      &\epsilon^0:& \ \frac{\d^2 c_0}{\d x^2} = 0, \
                    c_0(0)=0, c_0(1)=1 \ \Rightarrow c_0(x) = x
                    \label{eq:p11}\\
      &\epsilon^1:& \ \frac{\d^2 c_1}{\d x^2} + x^2 = 0, \
                    c_1(0)=0, c_1(1)=0 \ \Rightarrow c_1(x) = \frac{x-x^4}{12}
                    \nonumber \\ \label{eq:p12}\\
      &\vdots& \nonumber
    \end{eqnarray}
  }
\item{
	Collect the terms in accordance with Equation (\ref{eq:pertass1}), here
    to first order in $\epsilon$
    \begin{equation}
      c(x) = x + \frac{\epsilon}{12}(x-x^4) + \dots
      \end{equation}
    }
	This is the solution from the regular perturbation method; we here stop at first order already, but 
	one can continue (see the exercises). 
\end{enumerate}    

\begin{wrapfigure}{r}{0.4\textwidth}
	\includegraphics[width=0.35\textwidth]{figs/perturb_one.eps}
\end{wrapfigure}


In this example, the first order term, a measure of the effects from the reaction,
has a pre-factor of $\epsilon/12$. Therefore, the reaction process has only a small effect 
on the concentration profile even if $\epsilon$ is much larger than zero (and we move away from 
the regime $D \gg k$). 

The truncated power series above is formally known as the \emph{Poincar\'{e} expansion}
\index{Poincar\'{e} expansion} and
generally as an \emph{asymptotic expansion} \index{Asymptodic expansion}, and the approximation is written as
\begin{eqnarray}
	c(x) \sim \sum_{n=0}^N \epsilon^n c_n(x)  \, .
\end{eqnarray}
The \emph{remainder} can now be defined as 
\begin{eqnarray}
	R_N(x,\epsilon) = c(x) - \sum_{n=0}^N \epsilon^n c_n(x)  
\end{eqnarray}
and is a measure for the difference between the actual solution and the Poincar\'{e} expansion. Often 
we do not have an analytical expression for $c$ itself, but have to use a numerical computation. 
We can now define what we mean by the term well-approximated that we used 
above: The Poincar\'{e} expansion (with $N$ terms) is an asymptotic approximation to $c$ if for
all $M\leq N$ and $x \in [0; L]$ we have
\begin{eqnarray}
	\lim_{\epsilon \rightarrow 0}	\frac{R_M(x, \epsilon)}{\epsilon^M c_M(x)} = 0  \ 
	\text{for any fixed $x \in [0;L]$} \ .
	\label{eq:asymappr}
\end{eqnarray}
In words: We want the remainder to go to zero as $\epsilon$ goes to zero and faster than the last
term in the expansion. The asymptotic approximation is, likely, 
best illustrated with an example where the solution is known. 
\begin{example}
    The perturbation method is applicable for different types of non-linear
	problems, not only differential equations. Consider the quadratic problem 
	(example from Hince \textit{Perturbation Methods})
	\begin{equation}
 	 \label{eq:quadratic}
	  c^2 - \epsilon c -1 = 0 \, .
	\end{equation}
	Proposition: The Poincar\'{e} expansion
	\begin{eqnarray}
		c \sim c_0 + \epsilon c_1 + \epsilon^2c_2 
	\end{eqnarray}
	is an asymptotic approximation to the quadratic problem, Equation (\ref{eq:quadratic}).

	First, the solution is found from the quadratic formula
	\begin{eqnarray}
		\label{eq:solquadratic}
		c = \frac{1}{2} \left(\epsilon \pm \sqrt{\epsilon^2+4}\right) \, .
	\end{eqnarray}
	Notice that for $\epsilon = 0$	we have $c=\pm 1$. 
	Substituting the expansion and collecting the terms we have up to
	second order 
	\begin{eqnarray}
  	&\epsilon^0:& c_0^2 - 1 = 0 \ \Rightarrow \ c_0 = \pm 1 \\
  	&\epsilon^1:& 2 c_0c_1 - c_0= 0 \ \Rightarrow \ 
                \left\{
                \begin{matrix}
                  2c_1-1  = 0 \\
                  -2c_1 + 1 = 0
                \end{matrix}
  \right. \ \Rightarrow \ c_1 = \frac{1}{2} \\
  &\epsilon^2:& 2c_0c_2 + c_1^2-c_1 = 0
                \ \Rightarrow \ 
                \left\{
                \begin{matrix}
                  2c_2- \frac{1}{4}  = 0 \\
                  2c_2 + \frac{1}{4}= 0
                \end{matrix}
  \right. \ \Rightarrow \ c_2 = \pm \frac{1}{8} 
\end{eqnarray}
hence, the roots to second order in perturbation parameter are
	\begin{equation}
	  \pm 1 + \frac{\epsilon}{2} \pm \frac{\epsilon^2}{8} \, . 
	\end{equation}
	We have to show the limits in Equation (\ref{eq:asymappr}) (noting that $x$ is 
	not in the current problem). 

	For $M=0$ we have $c_0= \pm 1$ and 
	\begin{equation}
		R_0(\epsilon) = \frac{1}{2}(\epsilon \pm \sqrt{\epsilon^2 + 4}) \mp 1
	\end{equation}
	quickly see that
	\begin{eqnarray}
		\lim_{\epsilon \rightarrow 0} \frac{R_0(\epsilon)}{c_0} = \lim_{\epsilon
		\rightarrow 0} \frac{1}{2}(\epsilon \mp \sqrt{\epsilon^2 + 4}) \pm 	1 =
		0.
	\end{eqnarray}
	For $M=1$
	\begin{eqnarray}
		\lim_{\epsilon \rightarrow 0} \frac{R_1(\epsilon)}{\epsilon c_1} 
		=\lim_{\epsilon \rightarrow 0} \frac{\pm \sqrt{\epsilon^2+4} \mp 2}{\epsilon} 
		= \lim_{\epsilon \rightarrow 0} \frac{\epsilon}{\sqrt{\epsilon^2+4}} = 0 \, .
    \end{eqnarray}
	Last limit is found from L'Hopital's rule. Finally, for $M=2$ we have 
	\begin{eqnarray}
		\lim_{\epsilon \rightarrow 0} \frac{R_2(\epsilon)}{\epsilon^2 c_2} 
		= \lim_{\epsilon \rightarrow 0} \frac{\pm 4\sqrt{\epsilon^2 +4} \mp 8 \mp \epsilon^2}{\epsilon^2} 
		= 0 \, ,
	\end{eqnarray}
	applying L'Hoptial's rule twice. Thus, the Poincar\'{e} expansion with $N=2$ is an 
	asymptotic approximation to the quadratic equation, Equation (\ref{eq:quadratic}). 
\end{example}

\noindent Be aware, the perturbation method may not always
be helpful, at least not without performing additional tricks. Consider a
slightly different quadratic equation from above  
\begin{equation}
  \epsilon c^2 -c -1 = 0 \, .
\end{equation}
(Again, an example from Hince.) We immediately see that when $\epsilon=0$ 
there is only one solution, but for $\epsilon \neq 0$ there are two. 
A problem where the solution
shows such qualitative change setting $\epsilon=0$ is called a
\emph{singular perturbation problem}\index{Perturbation!singular}. Equation (\ref{eq:quadratic}) above 
is a regular perturbation problem since the number of
solutions did not change by varying $\epsilon$ around zero.
Performing the same steps as we did for regular problems,  
some times called \emph{the outer solution} \index{Perturbation!outer solution} 
when we deal with singular problems, we end up with the expansion
\begin{equation}
  c = 1 -\epsilon + 2\epsilon^2 + \ldots
\end{equation}
Compare this to the quadratic formula
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:quadricformulaPerturb}
  c = \frac{1}{2\epsilon}(1 \pm \sqrt{1-4\epsilon}) \ \ \epsilon  \neq 0 \, .
\end{equation}
These are two very different results, and since we trust the latter we must disregard the 
simple Poincar\'{e} expansion. 

Singular perturbation problems are not just far-fetched curiosities. 
Consider the steady-state RD-equation in the reaction dominated regime, $k \gg D$. 
Here we can introduce the perturbation parameter $\epsilon = D/k$ and we can write
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:second-singular}
  \epsilon \frac{\d^2c}{\d x^2} + c^2 = 0 \, . 
\end{equation}
As $\epsilon \rightarrow 0$ the diffusion term vanishes and the problem is algebraic only 
allowing $c=0$ for $0 < x < L$; the system qualitative behavior changes dramatically as we 
approach the limit $\epsilon \rightarrow 0$. This is singular perturbation problem. 
We will later encounter such problem, and the naive outer solution will, in
fact, be very helpful here.

\section{Chemotaxis \index{Chemotaxis}}
Experimentally it has been observed that some cells, e.g., E-coli, can register 
a chemical concentration gradient and move either down or up along this gradient. 
The chemical compound can be a hormone and cell motion is realised by, for example, flagella propulsion. 
This phenomenon is denoted \emph{chemotaxis}\index{Chemotaxis}. To model chemotaxis we need to
change the fluxes in the system. If diffusion and chemotaxis are independent processes we
can write the flux with two terms: (i) a term describing the usual diffusive flux 
and (ii) a term describing the flux due to chemotaxis. 

Consider a cell population with concentration $c_1=c_1(x,t)$ mixed with a
a chemical compound having concentration $c_2=c_2(x,t)$. The \emph{Keller-Segel
model} \index{Chemotaxis!Keller-Segel model} for the flux of the cells reads
\begin{eqnarray}
	J_1 = \chi c_1 \frac{\partial c_2}{\partial x} - D \frac{\partial c_1}{\partial x} \, ,
\end{eqnarray}
where $\chi$ is the \emph{chemotactic strength} \index{Chemotaxis!strength} and $D$ the usual diffusion 
coefficient for the cells. What the two terms describe should be clear. If the chemotactic strength 
or the gradient of the chemical is zero, then, of course, no flux occurs due to chemotaxis. 
The product in the chemotaxis term between $c_1$ and $\partial c_2/\partial x$ give raise to a 
(potential) non-linearity, and ensures that there is no flux of cells at point when there are no
cells even if the gradient of the chemical is non-zero. 

Substitution into the balance equation for $c_1$ gives 	
\begin{eqnarray}
	\frac{\partial c_1}{\partial t} = r(c_1) - \chi \frac{\partial}{\partial x} \left( c_1 \frac{\partial c_2}{\partial x} \right)
	+ D \frac{\partial^2c_1}{\partial x^2} 
\end{eqnarray}
if $\chi$ and $D$ are independent of spatial coordinate $x$. In the steady-state this is simply
\begin{eqnarray}
	r(c_1) -\chi \frac{\d}{\d x} \left( c_1 \frac{\d c_2}{\d x}\right) + D\frac{\d ^2 c_1}{\d x^2} = 0 \, . 
\end{eqnarray}

\begin{question}
	Will the cells move up or down the chemical gradient if $\chi > 0$? What if $\chi <	0$?
\end{question}

\begin{example}
	\label{ex:chemotaxis}
	Assume that we some how  can control the local concentration of the chemical compound.
	For example, let
	\begin{eqnarray}
		\label{eq:c2}
		c_2 = Kx/L  \ \text{such that} \ c_2(0)=0 \ \text{and} \ c_2(L) = K.
	\end{eqnarray}
	Let the cells feature a linear growth rate, $r = k c_1$, and use Dirichlet
	BCs $c_1(0) = C_0$ and $c_1(L) = 2C_0$, where $C_0 \neq 0$.  
	The equation (for the steady-state) is 	obtained using the product rule
	\begin{eqnarray}
		k c_1 -\chi \frac{\d}{\d x} \left( c_1 \frac{\d c_2}{\d x}\right) + D \frac{\d ^2 c_1}{\d x^2} = 
		0 \ \Rightarrow \ 
		\frac{k}{D} c_1 - \frac{\chi K}{D L} \frac{\d c_1}{\d x} +
		\frac{\d^2c_1}{\d x^2} =  0 \, . 
	\end{eqnarray}
	Define the parameter $\alpha = \chi K/(D L)$ then the characteristic polynomial is 
	\begin{eqnarray}
		\lambda^2 - \alpha \lambda + \frac{k}{D} = 0 \ ,
	\end{eqnarray}
	\begin{wrapfigure}{l}{0.4\textwidth}
		\includegraphics[width=0.35\textwidth]{figs/chemotaxis.eps}
	\end{wrapfigure}
	implying that 
	\begin{eqnarray}
		\lambda_{1,2} = \frac{1}{2}\left(
		\alpha \pm \sqrt{\alpha^2 - 4k/D}  \right) \, .
	\end{eqnarray}
	For real eigenvalues, $\alpha^2 > 4k/D$, we then have the general solution
	\begin{eqnarray}
		c_1(x) = A e^{\lambda_1 x} + B e^{\lambda_2 x} \, .
	\end{eqnarray}
	Applying the BCs we obtain
	\begin{eqnarray}
		c_1(x) = C_0 \left[
			e^{\lambda_1x} + \frac{2 - e^{\lambda_1 L}}{e^{\lambda_2L}-e^{\lambda_1 L}} 
			(e^{\lambda_2 x} - e^{\lambda_1 x})
		\right] \, . \nonumber \\
	\end{eqnarray}
	Note, by introducing chemotaxis the concentration profile can change from a
	concave function to a convex function depending on the sign of $\alpha$,
	that is, on the sign of the chemotactic strength, $\chi$.
\end{example}

In the general case, we may not be able to control the concentration of the
chemical compound and we then need a dynamical equation for $c_2$ as well.
Equation (\ref{eq:c2}) is equivalent to the case, where the chemical is not
consumed by the cells and simply diffuses according to Fick's law, that is,
$c_2$ follows the Laplace equation 
\begin{eqnarray}
	\frac{\d^2 c_2}{\d x^2} = 0 \ \text{with} \ c_2(0) = \ \text{and} \ c_2(L) =
	K \, .
\end{eqnarray}
We will not pursue the general case here.
